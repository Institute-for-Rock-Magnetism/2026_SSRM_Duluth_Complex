# Aeromagnetic anomalies of Minnesota

The magnetization of rocks is expressed at the scale of the state in aeromagnetic data. The interactive map below is built from the Minnesota Geological Survey's statewide aeromagnetic compilation, which merges surveys flown mainly between 1979 and 1991 (with contributing data back to 1961) into a seamless grid {cite}`Chandler1991a,Chandler2007a`. The Midcontinent Rift rocks that are the subject of this project — the Duluth Complex and the volcanic rocks that it intruded — produce the most prominent magnetic anomalies in the state, and this page uses the map to connect the rock magnetic properties measured on drill core D-6A to the crustal-scale magnetic field they generate.

## Total magnetic intensity

An aeromagnetic survey measures the scalar magnitude of the geomagnetic field along flight lines — typically flown at the lowest safe constant elevation (commonly ~150 m) perpendicular to the dominant geologic strike, with intensity measured to a precision of a few tenths of a nanotesla {cite}`Hinze2022a`. Subtracting a global reference field model leaves the **total magnetic intensity (TMI) anomaly** — the departure, in nanotesla (nT), of the measured field from the reference field. These anomalies are caused by the magnetization of rocks in the crust, and because the magnetic susceptibility of magnetite exceeds that of the common rock-forming silicates by several orders of magnitude, a TMI anomaly map is effectively a map of lateral contrasts in magnetite (and titanomagnetite) abundance and magnetization direction {cite}`Hinze2022a`. Anomalies over Minnesota span more than a thousand nanotesla — from lows near −560 nT to highs above +780 nT on the statewide compilation legend — against a background field of roughly 58,000 nT.

The anomaly pattern of the region is systematic. Moderate-amplitude highs of a few to several hundred nanotesla are associated with granite gneiss and highly metamorphosed rocks, lows of comparable amplitude with greenstone and sedimentary terranes, and long, narrow, high-intensity anomalies with steeply dipping iron formation, slate, tuff, and mafic dikes {cite}`Hinze2022a`. Because the Precambrian rocks of the region have been deformed such that contacts between units of contrasting magnetization are commonly steeply dipping, those contacts produce sharp, mappable anomalies — the property that makes aeromagnetic data so effective for mapping geology beneath the glacial cover {cite}`Hinze2022a`.

## The magnetic rocks of the Midcontinent Rift

The Midcontinent Rift dominates the anomaly map. High-intensity anomalies reaching several thousand nanotesla crudely follow the Lake Superior shoreline and continue southwest across the state beneath Phanerozoic cover, transecting the prevailing east-northeast anomaly grain of the Archean and Paleoproterozoic basement {cite}`Hinze2022a` — the magnetic expression of the same buried volcanic and intrusive rocks that make the rift traceable geophysically for ~2500 km {cite}`Miller2011b`. Unlike the surrounding regional anomalies, which arise from multiple basement sources, these anomalies are derived from the voluminous, mafic-dominated Mesoproterozoic volcanic rocks and associated intrusions of the rift, and their intensity is due largely to strong thermoremanent magnetization rather than induced magnetization alone {cite}`Hinze2022a`. Gravity and magnetic data have long been primary tools for mapping the poorly exposed interior of the Duluth Complex itself, where anomaly patterns distinguish the multiple anorthositic, troctolitic, and gabbroic intrusions that make up the complex {cite}`Chandler1990a`.

The rift is not the only strongly magnetic geology in the state. The east–west structural grain of the Archean Superior Province is visible across northern Minnesota, and the Biwabik Iron Formation of the Mesabi Iron Range — the same iron formation that occurs as a footwall unit and as assimilated inclusions in the South Kawishiwi intrusion — produces intense linear anomalies along its trend. That the D-6A stratigraphy contains both titanomagnetite-rich cumulates and iron-formation-derived oxide pods means that the units sampled by the core span much of the range of magnetization responsible for the anomalies on this map. The basal sulfide-mineralized units are a useful counterpoint: pyrrhotite, the magnetic Fe-sulfide that occurs in the mafic and ultramafic intrusions of the rift, has a susceptibility roughly an order of magnitude below that of magnetite, and the sulfide and oxide ores of the Duluth Complex are commonly not sufficiently magnetic to produce distinctive anomaly patterns of their own {cite}`Hinze2022a` — one reason the Cu-Ni deposits were found by drilling rather than by aeromagnetics.

## Induced and remanent magnetization and the Koenigsberger ratio

The magnetization that produces an anomaly is the vector sum of two contributions. **Induced magnetization** is carried in proportion to the present-day field and the rock's magnetic susceptibility, $M_i = \chi H$, and disappears if the field is removed. **Remanent magnetization** ($M_r$, the natural remanent magnetization or NRM) is locked into the rock — for the Duluth Complex, a thermoremanent magnetization acquired during cooling at ca. 1096 Ma — and is fixed in a direction that need not parallel the present field. Their relative importance is summarized by the **Koenigsberger ratio** {cite}`Koenigsberger1938a`:

$$Q = \frac{M_r}{M_i} = \frac{\mathrm{NRM}}{\chi H}$$

where $H$ is the local geomagnetic field strength (about 46 A/m in Minnesota, corresponding to ~58,000 nT). When $Q \ll 1$ the anomaly is dominated by induced magnetization and simply mirrors susceptibility; when $Q \gg 1$ the anomaly is dominated by the ancient remanence direction.

Compiled measurements from the Lake Superior region put numbers on this. $Q$ is less than 1 in most crystalline rocks but is commonly greater than 1 in volcanic rocks, and for the Mesoproterozoic rift volcanics $Q$ typically ranges between 1 and 3 with induced magnetizations of 1–3 A/m — so their remanence contribution equals or exceeds the induced one {cite}`Hinze2022a`. The directions compound the effect: normally polarized rift volcanic rocks carry remanence with an inclination near 40° and declination near 290°, reversely polarized rocks near −60° and 110°, while the present field in the region is inclined ~75° downward with ~3° declination — so modeling these anomalies requires the vector sum of the induced magnetization and a remanent magnetization that is oblique to it in every case {cite}`Hinze2022a`. Geometry matters even for purely induced sources: at the region's steep field inclination, an anomaly maximum sits slightly south of its source with a paired minimum to the north — the negative anomaly north of the Biwabik Iron Formation's positive anomaly is the classic local example — and remanence-dominated or reversely magnetized units depart from this pattern {cite}`Hinze2022a`.

This distinction matters for interpreting the rift anomalies. The main-stage Duluth Complex rocks acquired their remanence during a period of normal geomagnetic polarity, in a Mesoproterozoic field direction that differs from the present field, while early-stage rift rocks are reversely magnetized — so remanence can either reinforce or oppose the induced contribution, and strongly magnetic rocks can even produce anomaly lows where reversed-polarity remanence dominates. Interpretation of the gravity and magnetic data over the Duluth Complex has therefore had to account explicitly for remanent magnetization rather than treating anomalies as susceptibility maps {cite}`Chandler1990a`. The measurements made in this course connect directly to this ratio: the KT-10 and Kappabridge susceptibility data provide $\chi$, the magnetometer measurements provide the NRM, and together they give $Q$ for each specimen — making it possible to evaluate, unit by unit, whether the anomaly signature of the South Kawishiwi intrusion should be read as induced, remanent, or a mixture of the two.

## Reading the three views

- **Total field** — the TMI anomaly itself. Broad, deep, or strongly magnetized sources produce the large positive (brown) and negative (blue) anomalies. Clicking the map returns an anomaly estimate derived from the official legend calibration.
- **Tilt-enhanced (reduced to pole)** — reduction to the pole recomputes the anomaly as it would appear at the magnetic pole, where an anomaly sits directly above its source, and the tilt filter normalizes amplitude so that weak and strong sources are equally visible. This view emphasizes the edges and structural fabric of magnetic bodies — but the transformation is built on the assumption that the magnetization is entirely induced, parallel to the present-day field.
- **First vertical derivative** — the vertical gradient of the field, which suppresses broad deep-sourced anomalies and sharpens the expression of shallow sources; it is the view in which fine structural detail, such as the fabric within the Archean terranes and the trace of thin magnetic horizons, is most apparent.

That assumption deserves emphasis, because it fails most severely exactly where this map is most interesting. Reduction to the pole treats the total magnetization as induced — it does not consider remanent magnetization at all, which is equivalent to assuming $Q \approx 0$. For most of the Archean and Paleoproterozoic basement, where $Q$ is typically less than 1, the approximation is tolerable. But the Midcontinent Rift rocks that produce the strongest anomalies on the map carry $Q$ of 1–3 with remanence directions oblique (normal polarity: inclination ~40°) or roughly antiparallel (reversed polarity) to the steeply inclined present field {cite}`Hinze2022a`. For these rocks the majority of the signal violates the transformation's premise: anomaly maxima are displaced relative to their sources, reversely magnetized bodies can appear as spurious lows offset from spurious highs, and edges in the tilt view need not coincide with geologic contacts. The reduced-to-pole view is therefore at its least reliable directly over the rift — a caution to carry when comparing it against the total-field view, and one more reason the Koenigsberger ratio measured on the D-6A specimens is the essential quantity linking the core-scale measurements to the map.

## Data provenance and reproducibility

The map is a single self-contained HTML file with the raster views and legends embedded, so it makes no calls to external services. The source rasters exported from the Minnesota Geological Survey map service, the service metadata, and the scripts that recolor the rasters and assemble the standalone file are retained in [aeromagnetic/data/](aeromagnetic/data/SOURCE.md) and [aeromagnetic/tools/](aeromagnetic/tools/build_aeromagnetic.py). Full provenance — service URLs, layer numbers, export extent, and the recoloring procedure — is documented in [SOURCE.md](aeromagnetic/data/SOURCE.md).
