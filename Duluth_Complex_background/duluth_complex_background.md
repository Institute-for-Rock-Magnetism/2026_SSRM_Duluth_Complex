# Geologic background

## The Midcontinent Rift

The Midcontinent Rift is a protracted tectonomagmatic event in the interior of Laurentia, recorded by voluminous outpouring of lava and emplacement of intrusions accompanying rift development {cite}`Green1983a,Cannon1992a`. It is an arcuate intracontinental rift, traceable geophysically for ~2500 km from its exposure in the Lake Superior region southwest as far as eastern Kansas and southeast beneath the Michigan Basin {cite}`Miller2011b`. Magmatic activity initiated at ca. 1109 Ma and continued until ca. 1084 Ma, giving a duration of magmatism (~25 m.y.) that is longer than is typical of large igneous provinces {cite}`Swanson-Hysell2019a,Swanson-Hysell2021c`. While the overall duration was protracted, there were intervals of more voluminous magmatism, and the most voluminous period of magmatism occurred >10 m.y. after initial flood basalt volcanism during the interval known as the main magmatic stage. Preserved thicknesses of the volcanic rocks range from nearly 10 km for partial sections exposed on land to ~25 km beneath Lake Superior {cite}`Green1983a,Cannon1992a`. The magnetic expression of the rift — and the roles of induced and remanent magnetization in producing it — is explored on the [aeromagnetic anomalies of Minnesota](aeromagnetic_minnesota.md) page.

Rift magmatism is commonly subdivided into four stages {cite}`Miller2011b`. An **early stage** (ca. 1115–1107 Ma) is recorded by small mineralized ultramafic to mafic intrusions and by volcanics whose lower sections are primitive olivine tholeiites and whose upper sections are more evolved and show evidence of crustal contamination. A **latent stage** (ca. 1107–1100 Ma) is a hiatus in mafic magmatism during which the only rift-related rocks are felsic, interpreted as reflecting a period of underplating of the crust by mantle melts. The **main stage** (ca. 1100–1094 Ma) saw the volumetrically greatest magmatism, producing a bimodal distribution of tholeiitic compositions in which the mafic to intermediate rocks — though differentiated to varied extents — show little evidence of crustal contamination. A **late stage** (ca. 1094–1086 Ma) accompanied waning volcanism and continued subsidence.

Two features of this framework matter for the paleomagnetism of the Duluth Complex. Early stage magmatism occurred largely during an interval of reversed geomagnetic polarity and ended abruptly around 1107 Ma, whereas the main stage was characterized by normal polarity {cite}`Miller2011b`. The Duluth Complex layered and anorthositic series belong to the main stage, and so are expected to carry a normal-polarity primary remanence.

## The Duluth Complex

The Duluth Complex is one of the largest mafic intrusive complexes on Earth. With an arcuate exposed area of 5630 km², it is the second-largest exposed mafic intrusive complex on Earth after the Bushveld Complex {cite}`Miller2002c`. It is physically defined as an arcuate mass of semi-continuous mafic to felsic plutonic rocks extending from Duluth nearly to the Canadian border, bounded by a footwall of primarily Paleoproterozoic and Archean rocks and a hanging wall of mostly mafic volcanic rocks and hypabyssal intrusions {cite}`Miller2011b`. It was emplaced as sheet-like intrusions into the base of the comagmatic North Shore Volcanic Group, with the majority of its volume associated with the anorthositic series and the layered series of gabbroic and troctolitic cumulates {cite}`Miller2002c,Miller2002d,Miller2011b`. The geologic map in {numref}`duluth-complex-map` shows this arcuate geometry, the distribution of the layered, anorthositic, and early series, and the relationship of the complex to the North Shore Volcanic Group hanging wall and the Paleoproterozoic and Archean footwall.

{cite:t}`Miller2011b` distinguishes four rock series within the complex on the basis of age, dominant lithology, internal structure, and structural position: the **felsic series**, the **early gabbro series**, the **anorthositic series**, and the **layered series**. The felsic and early gabbro series were emplaced during early stage magmatism (ca. 1108 Ma), whereas the anorthositic and layered series were emplaced during the main stage. The layered series comprises at least 11 variably differentiated mafic layered intrusions that occur mostly along the base of the complex {cite}`Severson2016a`. The South Kawishiwi intrusion is one of these layered intrusions, and drill core D-6A is therefore a sample of the main-stage layered series.

### Rock nomenclature

Igneous rock types in the Duluth Complex have most commonly been classified by estimating the modal percentages of plagioclase, olivine, and pyroxene using a scheme modified from {cite:t}`Phinney1972a`. This is the classification underlying the rock-type codes in the D-6A log; the abbreviations are reproduced and defined in the [acronym glossary](../D-6A_log/D_6A_log_codes.md). Under this scheme the rocks of the complex consist dominantly of troctolite, augite troctolite, anorthositic troctolite, and — near the basal contact — norite, with local ultramafic layers ranging from melatroctolite to dunite.

The absence of a generally accepted classification scheme has been a persistent problem in describing these rocks, and the Phinney approach is not universally adopted: {cite:t}`Miller2011b` argues against modal schemes of this kind on the grounds that they employ modal boundaries that ignore the cotectic proportions of mineral phases expected to crystallize from basaltic magmas at low pressures. This is worth bearing in mind when the D-6A facies codes are treated as if they were petrologically natural divisions — they are a logging convention, and the boundaries between, for example, troctolite and picrite reflect modal cutoffs rather than sharp changes in crystallization behavior. Severson's own descriptions record that rock types can vary within a single stratigraphic unit on a hole-by-hole, interval-by-interval, and even geologist-by-geologist basis {cite}`Severson2016a`.

### Open-system behavior

The layered intrusions of the Duluth Complex differ in the degree to which they behaved as closed systems. The Layered Series at Duluth, the type intrusion of the complex, shows phase and cryptic layering indicating that it evolved as a relatively open system through periodic recharge and venting, in contrast to intrusions such as Sonju Lake that display a Skaergaard-type cumulate stratigraphy consistent with closed-system fractional crystallization {cite}`Miller2011b`. In the Layered Series at Duluth, macrocyclic phase layering is not accompanied by a complementary cryptic variation in mineral chemistry, and {cite:t}`Miller2011b` reports that this has been interpreted as predominantly related to devolatilization and decompression attending magma venting events from a shallow (<5 km) chamber, with magma recharge possibly playing a secondary role.

This bears on the interpretation of the ultramafic units in the South Kawishiwi intrusion described below, which have been interpreted as recording magma replenishment {cite}`Severson2016a`. Open-system behavior of either kind — recharge or venting — provides a mechanism for the repeated appearance of more primitive, olivine-rich lithologies within the troctolitic stratigraphy of D-6A, and those lithologies are precisely the ones expected to differ most in magnetic mineralogy.

### Age and rapid emplacement

Pioneering geochronology on the complex established its ca. 1.1 Ga age {cite}`Paces1993a`, and the complex as a whole has been described as emplaced between ca. 1108 and 1098 Ma, with the anorthositic and layered series placed at ca. 1099 Ma {cite}`Miller2011b,Hoaglund2010a`. Higher-precision CA-ID-TIMS ²⁰⁶Pb/²³⁸U zircon dates subsequently showed that the anorthositic and layered series were emplaced ca. 1096 Ma in less than one million years, with a duration of overall emplacement of 500 ± 260 k.y. {cite}`Swanson-Hysell2021c`. The oldest of those dates, 1096.19 ± 0.19 Ma, comes from an augite troctolite of the Partridge River intrusion at the base of the complex, and the youngest, 1095.69 ± 0.18 Ma, from a gabbroic anorthosite of the anorthositic series; the dates from the layered and anorthositic series are within uncertainty of one another {cite}`Swanson-Hysell2021c`. This emplacement was coeval with eruption of the upper southwest sequence of the North Shore Volcanic Group, which comprises ~7900 m of lava and is the thickest exposed part of the Midcontinent Rift volcanic succession. Taken together, these constraints require an exceptional thermal anomaly to have led to rapid and voluminous melt generation during the main stage of rift development {cite}`Swanson-Hysell2021c`.

### Paleomagnetism

{cite:t}`Beck1970a` published paleomagnetic data from 37 sites in the layered series and 11 sites in the anorthositic series near Duluth. The site directions from the two series overlap, consistent with their overlapping U-Pb dates, and can be combined into a mean pole (35.6°N, 188.7°E, A₉₅ = 3.1°, N = 24, k = 92) after correction for the tilt of the complex toward Lake Superior {cite}`Swanson-Hysell2021c`. The position of this pole relative to a synthesized apparent polar wander path is consistent with a ca. 1096 Ma age for the layered and anorthositic series, and strengthens the correlation with the volcanics {cite}`Swanson-Hysell2021c`. The Duluth Complex therefore has an established primary remanence at the scale of the complex, which provides context for interpreting the remanence carried by the individual lithologic units of drill core D-6A.

## The South Kawishiwi intrusion

The South Kawishiwi intrusion (SKI) consists mainly of troctolitic cumulates and dips gently to the southeast. It is exposed in an arc-shaped band that extends from the Serpentine deposit on the southwest to the Spruce Road deposit on the northeast {cite}`Severson2016a`. Footwall rocks include the Paleoproterozoic Virginia Formation and Biwabik Iron Formation and the Archean Giants Range Batholith, the last of which is the dominant footwall rock type — and is the unit in which drill hole D-6A bottoms. The presence of Biwabik Iron Formation as inclusions from the Birch Lake deposit as far north as the Spruce Road deposit is consistent with the interpretation that the majority of the Paleoproterozoic units were assimilated and removed from the footwall during emplacement of the intrusion {cite}`Severson2016a`.

The basal stratigraphic section of the SKI is known in detail from abundant drill core and is subdivided into 17 units that are present over a strike length of 31 km {cite}`Severson1994a,Miller2002d`. These are the units that the D-6A log is hung on, and they are the basis for the stratigraphic-unit codes used throughout this repository. The lowermost units are unevenly distributed along strike in a compartmentalized fashion, which {cite:t}`Miller2002d` interpret as recording a complicated intrusive history.

Several features of the SKI igneous stratigraphy bear directly on the units intersected by D-6A {cite}`Severson2016a`:

- **Sulfide mineralization is concentrated in the basal units.** The vast majority of sulfide mineralization is confined to the Basal Heterogeneous (BH), Basal Augite Troctolite and Norite (BAN), Updip Wedge (UW), and Ultramafic 3 (U3) units. This is consistent with the Cu-Ni enrichment recorded by the Exxon assays in the basal part of D-6A.
- **The ultramafic units are marker beds recording magma replenishment.** Three horizons containing abundant cyclic ultramafic layers — the U1, U2, and U3 units — serve as major marker beds. {cite:t}`Severson2016a` interpret them as recording periods of rapid and continuous magma replenishment that crystallized more primitive ultramafic layers before mixing with the resident magma. A pegmatite-bearing unit (PEG), originally recognized by {cite:t}`Foose1984a`, is a further marker.
- **U3 contains massive oxide pods.** The U3 unit is distinctive in containing massive, titanomagnetite-rich oxide pods as well as recognizable inclusions of bedded Biwabik Iron Formation. The spatial correspondence between the U3 unit and footwall iron formation is consistent with the interpretation that most of the massive oxide pods are iron-rich restite produced by assimilation and partial melting of the iron formation {cite}`Severson2016a`. Given that titanomagnetite is the dominant carrier of both induced and remanent magnetization in these rocks, this interpretation makes U3 of particular rock magnetic interest.
- **PGE enrichment is concentrated in U3 and PEG.** The U3 unit contains the majority of high PGE values, with high values also present in the PEG unit and at the top of the BH unit.

Noritic rocks are common at the basal contact and adjacent to footwall inclusions elsewhere in the complex, and are attributed to silica contamination from assimilated footwall rocks {cite}`Severson2016a`. The BAN unit at the base of D-6A, immediately above the Giants Range Batholith, is therefore a candidate interval in which to look for a magnetic-mineralogical signature of contamination.

## Relevance to the rock magnetic characterization

The geologic framework above sets up several expectations that the rock magnetic measurements on D-6A can test. The units differ substantially in modal oxide content, with the ultramafic units — and U3 in particular, with its titanomagnetite-rich pods — expected to contrast with the anorthositic troctolite and with the granitic footwall. The olivine-rich lithologies are susceptible to serpentinization, which generates secondary magnetite and can overprint a primary magnetic mineralogy. The basal units carry the sulfide mineralization, raising the question of what the Fe-sulfides contribute to the magnetic signal. That the complex as a whole carries a well-resolved primary remanence {cite}`Beck1970a,Swanson-Hysell2021c` means that the remanence recorded by these units can be interpreted against an established reference.
